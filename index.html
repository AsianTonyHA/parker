Social media and teen mental health

Teens spend about 3.5 hours a day on social media. This site covers what the research says about that, the signs a friend might be struggling, and where to get help in Southern Nevada.

What the research says

In 2023 the U.S. Surgeon General issued an advisory on social media and youth mental health. It found that teens who spend more than three hours a day on social media face double the risk of depression and anxiety symptoms. It also concluded there is not enough evidence to call social media safe for young people.

The advisory lists benefits too. The three-hour finding shows a link between heavy use and symptoms, and it does not prove that social media causes them. The effect varies from person to person.

Risks the advisory points to

  • Heavy use is linked to double the risk of symptoms
  • 46% of teens say it hurts how they feel about their bodies
  • Not enough evidence yet to call it safe

Benefits the advisory lists

  • Community with people who share your identity or interests
  • Access to information
  • A place for self-expression

Signs a friend (or you) may need support

Everyone has bad days. Watch for changes that last or start to get in the way of school, friends, or sleep.

  • Pulling away from friends, family, or things they used to enjoy
  • Big changes in sleep or appetite
  • Constant worry, fear, or feeling on edge
  • Feeling hopeless, empty, or worthless
  • Trouble focusing at school or work
  • Sudden mood swings or irritability

Only a trained professional can diagnose a condition. If you are worried about someone, reach out.

How to help a friend

You don't need perfect words. Showing up counts.

  1. Ask directly

    Try "I've noticed you seem down lately. How are you doing?" Then listen. You don't have to fix anything.

  2. Say you care

    Tell them you're not going anywhere. Skip the blame, the comparisons, and "just cheer up."

  3. Point to support

    Offer to help them find a counselor, a doctor, or the NAMI HelpLine. Going with them to the first appointment can make it easier.

Free, confidential support is available. If you or someone else is in immediate danger, call 911.
